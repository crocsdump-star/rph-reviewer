Items 1–10 · 20%

Essentials of History

This section is not about memorizing dates. It's about a way of reading events: what keeps happening, what suddenly breaks, and why both matter.

What you're expected to do with history

Instead of just recalling facts, you should be able to:

  • Spot patterns across different time periods
  • Spot irregularities — things that break the pattern
  • Understand continuity and change together
  • Apply that thinking to situations happening right now
  • Explain why any of this matters for citizenship and your future job

Historical patterns

A pattern is something that keeps repeating — a structure, behavior, or process that shows up across different periods. Example: colonial powers reusing the same labor-control strategies, or an economic dependency that just changes hands from one administration to the next.

Ask yourselfWhat keeps happening?
PATTERN = RECURRENCE

Historical irregularities (anomalies)

An irregularity is the opposite: an unexpected event that interrupts a pattern that was already in place. A sudden policy reversal, a regulatory shock, an infrastructure failure that comes out of nowhere.

Ask yourselfWhat suddenly changed?
IRREGULARITY = INTERRUPTION

Why you need both, not just one

The average and difficult items reward students who look at pattern and irregularity together, not one or the other.

Worked example — the bridge A bridge has run safely for decades, then suddenly collapses. A weak answer only looks at the day it fell. A strong answer:
  1. Looks at the long-term maintenance pattern
  2. Identifies the one unusual event
  3. Figures out what caused the rupture
  4. Connects the long pattern to the sudden anomaly
Historical Analysis = Continuity/Pattern + Change/Irregularity + Context

Case in point: GOMBURZA

The exam uses the execution of GOMBURZA to test this exact logic. The point isn't to recall the date — it's to trace the reasoning chain:

GOMBURZA executions → looks like an isolated, harsh event → but shifts public consciousness → fuels nationalism

An event can be limited, sudden, and disruptive in the moment, and still produce long-term consequences. That's the insight to walk in with.

Why any of this matters for your career

The exam keeps translating historical thinking into workplace skills: contextual decision-making, ethical responsibility, analytical thinking, spotting patterns, spotting anomalies, understanding systems.

If a question asks "how is history relevant to your profession?"Look for the answer built on context + analysis + evidence + ethics + responsible decision-making — never one that's just about memorizing facts.

Histo-Competencies

Five skills the exam expects you to name, explain, and — in the hard questions — use all at once.

The five competencies

CompetencyMain questionKeyword
Critical ThinkingIs this interpretation sound?QUESTION
Information LiteracyCan this information be verified?VERIFY
Contextual AnalysisWhat were the circumstances of the time?CONTEXT
Objective Use of TheoriesAm I applying the framework fairly?OBJECTIVITY
Logical CommunicationCan I clearly demonstrate my conclusion?ARGUE
QUESTION → VERIFY → CONTEXTUALIZE → APPLY FAIRLY → COMMUNICATE

1. Critical thinking

Examining information carefully before you accept its conclusion — checking for bias, questioning claims, comparing interpretations instead of jumping to one.

ExampleA colleague presents historical data while clearly favoring one product. Correct move: identify the possible bias before accepting the interpretation.
CRITICAL THINKING = QUESTION

2. Information literacy

Finding, evaluating, comparing, and verifying sources — telling reliable information apart from unreliable information.

ExampleA viral historical post has no citation. Correct move: find, evaluate, and compare reliable sources — not share it, not memorize it, not just have an opinion about it.
INFORMATION LITERACY = VERIFY

3. Contextual analysis

Understanding an event or document within the conditions of its own time, instead of judging it by today's standards.

ExampleA lawyer reviews Spanish-era land documents. They must avoid reading modern property concepts into a document that comes from a completely different legal and social context.

Presentism is the trap this skill guards against — judging the past mainly by present-day standards without weighing historical circumstances enough.

CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS = TIME + SITUATION

4. Objective use of theories

Applying a framework fairly, systematically, and consistently — not letting personal beliefs distort the analysis. This does not mean "never criticize anything." It means apply the same standard evenly.

OBJECTIVE THEORIES = FAIR APPLICATION

5. Logical communication / research diligence

Finding information isn't the finish line. You need to organize evidence, explain your reasoning, build an argument, and back claims with credible evidence.

LOGICAL COMMUNICATION = EXPLAIN + SUPPORT

Putting all five together

Hard questions require integration. Here's the sequence the exam expects when you're handed a contested historical claim:

  1. Critical thinking — Who's making the claim? What assumptions or interests are behind it?
  2. Information literacy — What sources support or contradict it?
  3. Contextual analysis — What historical circumstances produced this claim?
  4. Objective theory — What appropriate framework can analyze it fairly?
  5. Logical communication — Present a structured, evidence-based conclusion.

This exact chain shows up in the difficult items about social-media historical distortion and corporate due diligence.

Case in point: the Tejeros Convention

Used as the application case for all five competencies. When electoral anomalies from 1897 come up, the weak question is "who won?" The strong question the exam rewards is:

How do we evaluate the evidence and circumstances surrounding the event?

That means weighing primary accounts, verifying sources, understanding 1897's electoral framework, comparing competing interpretations, and reaching an evidence-based conclusion — all five competencies at once.

Sources & Cartography

How to classify a historical source, trace where an artifact came from, and read the 1734 Murillo Velarde Map as more than just a map.

Primary, secondary, and hybrid sources

TypeMain characteristic
PrimaryDirect or contemporary evidence — original land grants, cadastral records, firsthand accounts, contemporary photographs
SecondaryA later interpretation or analysis — journal articles, historians' analysis, textbooks
HybridDifferent layers of evidence or authorship combined in one document
Primary exampleFor a Spanish-period land claim, the most direct evidence is the original land grant or colonial cadastral record from that period.
Secondary exampleA modern scholarly article analyzing the 1897 Tejeros Convention is a secondary source — it's an interpretation written well after the fact.
Hybrid exampleA 1942 guerrilla diary that also has later annotations added by a descendant. You don't treat the whole thing as one uniform source — you separate what's original from what was added later.

Provenance

Provenance is information about an artifact's origin, history, ownership, creation, and documentation. When you're handed an artifact or photograph, ask:

  • Who created it, and when?
  • Where did it come from, and who possessed it?
  • Can its history actually be traced?
  • Is the attribution credible?

This matters most in the difficult local-history items about photographs and museum artifacts.

The Murillo Velarde Map (1734)

Don't study this as "just a map." The exam wants you to see it as a political instrument — colonial cartography served imperial expansion, administrative control, and territorial claims, not just navigation.

Maps can be political instruments.

Reading the map critically — avoid both extremes

Extreme 1 — wrong"The map is colonial, therefore it's useless."
Extreme 2 — also wrong"The map is historical, therefore everything on it is objective truth."

The better approach: analyze it critically while still preserving its historical value — look at its colonial purpose, imperial interests, territorial claims, and its marginal illustrations, which can hold evidence of indigenous presence.

Indigenous representation in the map's margins

Don't automatically privilege only the colonial administrative point of view. The map's marginal illustrations can reveal indigenous livelihoods, cultural practices, social life, and identity — and the exam ties this reading to the principles in CMO No. 2, Series of 2019.

Items 31–40 · 20%

Oral History

Centered on the Tinguian narrative of Apobolinayen, and on the line between a meaningful tradition and a verified fact.

What oral history actually is

Collecting and analyzing people's remembered experiences, narratives, testimonies, and traditions. It can surface cultural knowledge, social insight, spiritual worldview, community memory, and voices that official records leave out.

Apobolinayen

This Tinguian narrative is the exam's main example. It's meant to offer insight into Tinguian identity, worldview, culture, social practices, and cosmology — not to be read as mere entertainment.

Oral tradition ≠ automatically historical fact

This is the distinction the exam tests most directly. An oral tradition can be historically valuable without every element being literally true.

ExampleA narrative describes a supernatural or celestial encounter. The correct historical approach: recognize it as folklore or symbolic narrative — not automatically treat it as literal historical fact.
CategoryWhat it means
Historical factA claim supported through credible evidence and verification
Folklore / local mythologyNarratives with symbolic, supernatural, cultural, or traditional elements
Oral historyInformation passed through testimony, memory, experience, and community tradition
Respect the cultural value of a narrative without automatically declaring every detail historically verified.

Triangulation

Comparing information across multiple sources or forms of evidence to strengthen or test a historical claim.

ExampleElders describe a pre-colonial trade practice. Don't rely on one person's testimony — compare it against oral testimony + historical documentation + anthropology + material evidence.
TRIANGULATION = MULTIPLE EVIDENCE

Ethical oral history

Research with Indigenous communities has to respect community consent, intellectual property, cultural protocols, community participation, and narrative authority. Researchers do not automatically have the right to record everything, publish everything, reinterpret everything, or turn oral tradition into text without consultation.

RESPECT + RECORD + VERIFY + CONTEXTUALIZE + CONSENT
Items 41–50 · 20%

Local History

How national history plays out in one specific town, barangay, or community — and how to tell a verified local fact from a nice-sounding legend.

What local history is for

It studies a specific town, city, municipality, barangay, or community — but it should never be treated in isolation from national history.

Local history grounds national history in the experience of particular places.

The exam frames local history as showing how national developments were experienced, resisted, adapted, and reshaped locally.

Localization

Examining how a broader historical process actually played out in one particular locality.

Weak framing"Spanish colonial policies affected the Philippines."
Localized framing"How were those policies implemented in this particular town?" — then dig into local institutions, land arrangements, buildings, population, economic activity, resistance, and adaptation.

Local etymology / toponymy

Studying where a town or barangay name came from. Never rely on just one story.

Strong combination: local government records + published local histories + oral interviews.

An interesting legend is not automatically an established fact.

Handling local legends

ScenarioAn elder says: "Our barangay was named after a miraculous event."

The correct response is neither to accept it immediately nor reject it immediately. Instead: cross-check the explanation against documentary and other available evidence.

Local heritage

Churches, municipal buildings, ancestral structures, monuments, landscapes, community spaces, photographs, and maps can all carry historical and cultural significance — even without a nationally famous hero attached. These places contribute to community identity.

Judging whether an old photo is real

An old-looking photograph is not automatically authentic. Apply three checks:

  1. Actual — Does it really depict what it claims to depict?
  2. Authentic — Is it genuinely from the claimed period or source?
  3. Verified — Can its identification and provenance be supported?

For provenance, look for the source institution, creator or photographer, date, location, original collection, acquisition history, and a traceable repository. A photo without provenance shouldn't be presented as authentic just because it "looks old."

Periodization

For deeper local-history analysis, organize development into periods — for example, Spanish colonial → American → contemporary — and trace how administration, land ownership, socioeconomic structures, institutions, settlement, and infrastructure changed across them. The exam favors periodization combined with analysis, not just a list of dates.

Community storytelling

Community storytelling has real historical value — it preserves memory, recovers marginalized voices, strengthens community identity, and supplements official records. But that doesn't mean every personal memory is automatically accurate. Value participation while keeping source-critical standards.

The 5-step process for difficult items

Difficult questions share one trait: the correct answer combines several principles instead of applying just one.

  1. Identify the historical problem — What is the question actually asking?
  2. Identify the evidence — What sources or information are given?
  3. Identify the competency — Critical thinking? Information literacy? Contextual analysis? Objective theory? Logical communication?
  4. Look for integration — Does the best answer combine more than one approach?
  5. Reject extreme answers — Be suspicious of choices that accept everything, reject everything, rely on one source, ignore context, invent evidence, prioritize convenience, or confuse legend with fact.

The final master concept

Good historical thinking doesn't just collect information. It evaluates evidence, understands context, recognizes patterns and changes, respects different forms of historical knowledge, verifies claims, and communicates conclusions responsibly. That's why the exam moves from historical patterns → histo-competencies → sources → oral traditions → local history: each area builds toward actually doing historical analysis, not just understanding it.
